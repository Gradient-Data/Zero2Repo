# Dynamical Systems Library — Full Product Requirements

The Dynamical Systems Library supports modeling, analysis, simulation, and design of feedback systems, including linear, nonlinear, continuous-time, and discrete-time systems.

### FP-01: Linear input/output system models

- Callers can describe single-input, single-output and multi-input, multi-output linear systems using real state-space matrices or real numerator and denominator polynomials for each input/output channel. Single-channel systems can also be described by zeros, poles, and gain, with conjugate pairs for nonreal roots. The resulting model exposes its dimensions and supplied dynamics, including static gains and transfer functions whose numerator degree exceeds their denominator degree. Incompatible matrix dimensions, inconsistent channel counts, and an identically zero denominator are refused as invalid model descriptions.

- Models distinguish continuous time, discrete time with a specified positive sampling period, discrete time with an unspecified sampling period, and an unspecified timebase. Dynamic models default to continuous time; static state-space and constant transfer-function models default to an unspecified timebase. Combining an unspecified timebase with another timebase adopts the other timebase; unspecified discrete time adopts the other discrete sampling period. Continuous and discrete time cannot be combined, and conflicting specified sampling periods are refused as incompatible.

- Callers can name systems and their input, output, and state signals, inspect those names and counts, and select linear input/output subsystems by channel position or signal name. Selection preserves the selected channel behavior and signal names in the requested order. Signal-name counts inconsistent with model dimensions and selections of nonexistent signals are refused as invalid signal descriptions.

- At caller-supplied complex points away from poles, state-space and transfer-function models return the complex transfer value for every selected input/output channel. A channel transfer function is the quotient of its numerator and denominator polynomials; a zero-pole-gain description is the gain times the product of differences from its zeros divided by the product of differences from its poles. State-space models return the state derivative or next state as the sum of the state matrix applied to the current state and the input matrix applied to the current input. Output is the sum of the output matrix applied to the state and the feedthrough matrix applied to the input. Transfer evaluation represents these same linear dynamics with zero initial state.

- Callers can construct frequency response data models from complex measurements and their frequencies, preserving every input/output channel and sample. Frequency counts inconsistent with the response samples are refused. Evaluation at an unrecorded frequency is refused unless interpolation was requested. With distinct increasing sample frequencies, an interpolating model provides a continuous complex response within the sampled interval and reproduces the supplied samples; constant or affine complex data remain constant or affine between samples. Interpolation requires more than one frequency sample.

- Random linear models have the requested positive numbers of states, inputs, and outputs and are stable: continuous-time poles have negative real parts and discrete-time poles lie inside the unit circle. Requesting a strictly proper model produces zero direct feedthrough. Nonpositive or nonintegral model dimensions are refused.

### FP-02: Trajectory basis families

- Callers can represent time-dependent trajectories using polynomial powers, Bezier curves, or B-splines, and evaluate basis values, requested derivatives, and trajectories specified by coefficients at supplied times. A trajectory is the sum of its coefficients times the corresponding basis values. Polynomial powers start at the constant term and ascend through degree one less than the number of basis elements. Bezier coefficients weight the Bernstein polynomial basis of that same degree, ordered from the initial to the final endpoint. Both families use time divided by the caller's positive duration, defaulting to one time unit; derivatives are with respect to the original time.

- B-spline trajectories use the normalized, endpoint-clamped B-spline basis on the supplied breakpoints and requested degree, with continuity through the requested number of derivatives at interior breakpoints. The endpoint knot multiplicity is one more than the degree and each interior knot multiplicity is the degree minus the requested smoothness. Omitted smoothness means continuity through one fewer derivative than the degree. Multiple trajectory variables may have different degrees and smoothness on shared breakpoints; evaluation keeps their coefficient sets and values distinct. Derivative evaluation is supported through the specified smoothness across the whole interval.

- B-splines require at least two strictly increasing breakpoints, integer degree of at least one, and integer smoothness from zero through one less than the degree. Invalid breakpoints, degrees, smoothness, or per-variable specification lengths are refused. Separate breakpoint sequences for different variables are refused as unsupported.

- Callers can supply an additional trajectory basis by defining its values and derivatives. Coefficient-based trajectory evaluation uses those supplied basis values, allowing a trajectory family beyond the three built-in families.

### FP-03: Nonlinear input/output system models

- Callers can define continuous-time state derivatives or discrete-time state updates and output laws that depend on time, state, input, and named model parameters. Evaluating a model returns the values of the supplied laws. Models support multiple inputs, outputs, and states, and carry caller-assigned signal names and timebase information.

- A dynamic nonlinear model without an output law returns its state as its output, unless the caller explicitly requests zero outputs, in which case it returns no output signals. A static nonlinear model has no dynamic state and evaluates its output from the current input. Requesting static input-only evaluation of a model with dynamic state is refused as unsupported.

- Model parameters have caller-supplied defaults. Parameter overrides supplied for an evaluation or analysis replace the corresponding default values while retaining unspecified defaults, so the result reflects the requested parameter values. Overrides do not change the defaults used by later calls.

- A model declared static cannot also declare dynamic states. A model without an output law must declare either no outputs or as many outputs as states. These conflicting descriptions are refused as invalid models.

### FP-04: Block diagrams and signal interconnections

- Compatible linear and nonlinear systems can be connected in series, in parallel with summed outputs, or with negated outputs. Independent appending supports linear systems. Scalar gains and compatible constant gain matrices participate in these connections. The combined model produces the response of the specified signal routing, and mismatched connected dimensions are refused.

- Feedback connections support negative feedback by default. State-space and single-input, single-output transfer-function connections also support caller-selected positive feedback. State-space feedback supports multiple channels; direct transfer-function feedback supports single-input, single-output systems and refuses multiple-channel transfer-function feedback as unsupported. A state-space feedback connection with singular instantaneous feedback equations is refused as an ill-posed connection.

- Callers can build networks of named subsystems by routing weighted subsystem outputs to subsystem inputs and choosing external inputs and outputs. Multiple contributions to one input are summed, and an external input can drive several subsystem inputs. A network of linear state-space subsystems yields a linear model; a network containing nonlinear dynamics retains those dynamics. Referenced signals that do not exist and incompatible connection dimensions are refused.

- When explicit internal connections are omitted, subsystem inputs receive the sum of subsystem outputs with the same signal name. Inputs sharing a name receive the same contributions. Unconnected subsystem inputs receive zero, and unused subsystem inputs or outputs are reported unless the caller explicitly suppresses or exempts them; the report identifies which signals remain unused.

- Summing junctions combine scalar or equal-length vector signals with caller-selected signs. Transfer-function arithmetic additionally supports single-channel division and integer powers, and callers can assemble a transfer matrix from individual transfer functions or separate it into its channel transfer functions while retaining their behavior.

- Frequency response data models support series, parallel, and negative-feedback arithmetic on compatible sampled frequency grids, including combinations with linear models evaluated at those frequencies and constant gains. Incompatible frequency grids are refused. A nonlinear network with an unresolved instantaneous algebraic loop does not deliver a simulated response and reports an unsupported algebraic loop.

### FP-05: Model conversion and time discretization

- State-space models can be converted to transfer functions, and proper transfer functions can be converted to state-space models, preserving their input/output transfer behavior and timebase. Conversion of an improper transfer function to state space is refused as unsupported.

- A caller-supplied invertible change of state coordinates produces an equivalent state-space model. By default the supplied transformation maps old states to new states; callers can instead specify the reverse mapping. In continuous time, multiplying the time coordinate by a positive scale slows the dynamics by that scale, with a default scale of one. Reachable, observable, and modal representations return the transformed model and coordinate transformation. Reachable and observable forms require a single-input, single-output model with full-rank controllability or observability respectively, and refuse models that do not meet it. The default canonical representation is reachable.

- Continuous-time state-space and single-input, single-output transfer-function models can be converted to discrete-time models with the caller's sampling period. Supported conversion semantics are zero-order hold, first-order hold, impulse-invariant conversion, generalized bilinear transformation, bilinear transformation, forward difference, and backward difference; single-channel transfer functions additionally support matched poles and zeros. First-order hold represents inputs varying linearly between samples; impulse-invariant conversion preserves the sampled impulse response for strictly proper systems and refuses direct feedthrough. The default is zero-order hold, preserving the continuous system's sampled response to inputs held constant between samples. Already-discrete models and multiple-channel transfer-function sampling requests are refused.

- Generalized bilinear conversion accepts a weight from zero through one, with forward difference at zero, bilinear conversion at one half, and backward difference at one. A missing or out-of-range weight is refused. With bilinear conversion, a caller can select a prewarping frequency at which the discrete and continuous frequency responses agree in magnitude and phase. Requesting prewarping for another conversion warns that it is ignored; prewarping with bilinear conversion completes without a warning.

- A nonnegative pure time delay can be approximated by a rational transfer function with a requested nonnegative denominator degree and numerator degree between zero and that degree. The approximation matches the delay's expansion at zero frequency through the combined numerator and denominator degrees. The denominator degree defaults to one and the numerator degree defaults to the denominator degree; zero delay gives unity gain. Negative delays and degrees outside the allowed range are refused.

- The reachable representation uses a companion state matrix with the negated monic characteristic-polynomial coefficients in its first row, ordered by descending power, unit entries immediately below the diagonal, and zero elsewhere; its input acts on the first state with unit gain. The observable representation has the transposed companion pattern and observes its first state with unit gain. Their returned coordinate maps take old states to new states. Modal representation separates independent real modes and conjugate mode pairs into real diagonal blocks, allowing larger blocks for inseparable repeated modes; its returned map takes modal states back to original states. Input and output matrices preserve the corresponding state and input/output behavior.

- Generalized bilinear conversion has the frequency-variable relation in which the continuous variable is the discrete variable minus one divided by the sampling period times the weighted sum of the discrete variable and one; the weights are the supplied weight and its complement respectively. Matched conversion maps every pole and zero through the exponential of its continuous location times the sampling period and preserves the leading low-frequency gain, including when poles or zeros occur at the origin. Impulse-invariant conversion makes each discrete unit-sample impulse coefficient equal the sampling period times the corresponding continuous impulse sample.

### FP-06: State-space matrix analysis and equations

- Given compatible state and input matrices or state and output matrices, callers obtain the controllability or observability matrix for a requested positive integer horizon, defaulting to the state dimension and capped at that dimension. The controllability matrix concatenates the input matrix acted on by successive powers of the state matrix, starting with power zero. The observability matrix stacks the output matrix followed by its products with those same powers. At the full state-dimension horizon, rank equal to the state count identifies full controllability or observability. Incompatible matrix dimensions are refused.

- For stable continuous-time or discrete-time state-space systems, callers can obtain controllability and observability Gramians. The continuous controllability Gramian is the time integral of the state impulse-response matrix times its transpose; the observability Gramian is the time integral of the transpose of the zero-input initial-state-to-output map times that map. Both integrals run from zero to infinity. In discrete time the corresponding quantities are sums over all steps, using unit-sample impulse excitation for reachability and including the initial output in observability. Callers can also obtain a Gramian factor whose transpose times the factor reproduces the Gramian: controllability factors support input counts no greater than state counts, and observability factors support output counts no greater than state counts. Unstable systems are refused for these computations.

- Callers can solve continuous-time and discrete-time Lyapunov and Sylvester equations that have unique finite solutions, including generalized Lyapunov equations with a nonsingular descriptor matrix. Continuous Lyapunov solutions make the sum of the dynamics matrix times the solution, its transpose, and the symmetric forcing matrix zero. In generalized form the first product also has the descriptor transpose on its right. Discrete Lyapunov solutions equal the dynamics matrix times the solution times the dynamics transpose plus the forcing matrix; in generalized form the equality instead has the descriptor times the solution times the descriptor transpose on its left. Continuous Sylvester solutions make the left coefficient times the solution plus the solution times the right coefficient plus the forcing matrix zero. Discrete Sylvester solutions equal the left coefficient times the solution times the right coefficient transpose plus the forcing matrix. Incompatible dimensions, nonsymmetric Lyapunov forcing matrices, and simultaneous requests for a Sylvester equation and descriptor form are refused as invalid equations.

- Callers can solve continuous-time and discrete-time algebraic Riccati equations for state dynamics, input influence, symmetric state and input weights, an optional state-input cross weight, and an optional nonsingular descriptor matrix. The result contains the quadratic cost-to-go matrix, negative-feedback gain, and closed-loop eigenvalues of the dynamics minus input influence times gain relative to the descriptor. An omitted input weight in continuous time is identity; omitted cross weight is zero and omitted descriptor is identity. The default is the stabilizing solution; callers can also request the nonstabilizing branch when that finite branch exists. Invalid dimensions and required symmetry violations are refused; failure to obtain the requested solution is reported as an unsolved equation.

- The continuous Riccati solution makes zero the sum of the dynamics transpose times the solution times the descriptor, the transpose of that product, and the state weight, minus the feedback gain transpose times the input weight times the feedback gain. The input weight times the gain equals the input-influence transpose times the solution times the descriptor plus the cross-weight transpose. The discrete solution makes the descriptor transpose times the solution times the descriptor equal the dynamics transpose times the solution times the dynamics plus the state weight minus the gain transpose times the effective input weight times the gain. The effective input weight is the supplied input weight plus the input-influence transpose times the solution times the input influence; its product with the gain equals the input-influence transpose times the solution times the dynamics plus the cross-weight transpose.

### FP-07: Time response and transient characteristics

- For linear models, callers can obtain output and state trajectories from an initial condition with zero input, unit-step excitation, unit-area impulse excitation, or a supplied input history. Step and impulse responses distinguish excitation of each input and allow selection of the input and output channels. Omitted initial conditions are zero.

- Continuous-time forced linear response uses the supplied input samples with linear interpolation between times; its supplied time grid must be uniformly spaced. Discrete-time response respects a specified sampling period: output spacing cannot be shorter than that period and must be an integer multiple of it, with inputs interpolated linearly at intervening sample instants. Returned samples use the supplied times by default; callers can instead request samples at every intervening system sampling instant. For a discrete system with unspecified sampling period, a supplied uniform time grid sets the period; with no supplied time grid, successive input samples are one time unit apart. Inconsistent time, input, or initial-state dimensions and incompatible time spacing are refused.

- Nonlinear and interconnected models can be simulated from supplied initial states and input samples, with caller-selected parameter values. Continuous input samples are linearly interpolated, and callers can request output evaluation times separately from the input sample times. Discrete nonlinear simulation requires equally spaced times matching its sampling period. The result includes the simulated inputs, states, and outputs; failed integration is reported as a simulation failure, or as an unsuccessful response with a reason when failure-return behavior is explicitly requested.

- Step, impulse, and initial-condition response requests for linear models can omit the time grid; the result supplies an increasing grid and the corresponding response. A collection of models can share one time grid and one excitation while returning distinguishable responses for each model. A continuous-time impulse response with nonzero direct feedthrough warns that the instantaneous impulse component is absent from the finite sampled output; without direct feedthrough the impulse response is returned without a warning.

- Time response results retain the association between times, signals, excitation traces, and originating systems. Callers can combine responses on the same time grid into multiple traces and plot selected inputs and outputs separately or overlaid. Plotted coordinates represent the response data, and overlaid systems and traces remain distinguishable. Combining responses with incompatible time grids or signal dimensions is refused.

- For a step response that reaches a finite nonzero final value within the supplied observation interval, callers obtain rise time, settling time, minimum and maximum from the upper rise threshold onward including the final value, overshoot, undershoot, absolute peak, first peak time, and final value per input/output channel. Rise time defaults to the elapsed time between reaching 10 percent and 90 percent of the final value; each crossing is the first sample at or beyond its threshold in the direction of the final value; settling time is the first sampled time after which the response remains within 2 percent of that value. Both thresholds can be changed. Overshoot measures the largest excursion beyond the final value and undershoot the largest excursion in the opposite direction from the final value, each as a percentage of the absolute final value. The final value comes from the caller when supplied, otherwise from the model's steady-state gain or the last sample of supplied response data.

### FP-08: Frequency response and frequency-domain plots

- Linear models produce complex frequency response, magnitude, and phase for every requested input/output channel. Continuous models are evaluated on the imaginary axis; discrete models are evaluated on the unit circle at angles given by angular frequency times sampling period. Frequencies are reported in increasing order. Callers can supply frequencies, request a frequency interval and number of samples, or receive an automatically chosen increasing frequency grid. Evaluation above the discrete-time Nyquist frequency warns about that frequency-range violation; evaluation at frequencies below it gives no warning.

- Bode plots show magnitude and phase against frequency, Nichols plots show magnitude against phase for single-channel systems, and singular-value responses and plots show the transfer matrix's singular values at each frequency in descending order. Magnitude can be displayed as absolute gain or decibels, phase as radians or degrees, and frequency as angular frequency or cycles per time unit. Multiple systems and selected channels can be overlaid or separated while preserving their identities and numerical response values.

- Nyquist analysis of a single-channel linear model returns the evaluated contour, complex response, and net clockwise encirclement count around the critical point at negative unity gain, and can plot the response together with its negative-frequency branch. Contours can detour around poles on the stability boundary; continuous-time detours around imaginary-axis poles enter the right half-plane by default. A frequency contour that does not support an integer encirclement count or gives an inconsistent stability count produces a warning rather than an unqualified stability conclusion. A contour that does support a consistent integer count yields its count without a warning.

- Given a single-channel plant and controller in unity negative feedback, callers can obtain and plot sensitivity, complementary sensitivity, plant times sensitivity, and controller times sensitivity. Sensitivity is the reciprocal of one plus the plant-controller loop transfer; complementary sensitivity is the loop transfer times sensitivity. Multiple-channel inputs to this analysis are refused as unsupported.

- Frequency response data can be plotted directly without reconstructing a dynamical model. Plotting and numeric access retain input/output channel associations, including caller-assigned names. Requesting a single-channel-only plot for multiple-channel data is refused as unsupported.

### FP-09: Stability margins, gain measures, and passivity

- Callers can obtain the zero-frequency gain of linear models wherever that gain is finite. Single-channel bandwidth is the first frequency where magnitude falls below the requested decibel change from the zero-frequency gain, defaulting to a drop of 3 decibels. No crossing yields infinite bandwidth; infinite zero-frequency gain yields an undefined bandwidth result. A nonnegative requested decibel change and multiple-channel bandwidth requests are refused.

- Single-channel loop models or sampled frequency responses yield gain margins, phase margins, local minima of the distance of the Nyquist response from negative unity, and their frequencies, assuming unity negative feedback. Gain margin is the reciprocal loop magnitude at a negative-real-axis crossing. Phase margin is the signed rotation from the loop phase at a unity-magnitude crossing to the negative real axis, positive for additional tolerable phase lag and expressed within a half-turn in either direction. Callers can request all crossings and local minima or the limiting results. The limiting gain margin minimizes the absolute logarithm of the gain factor, the limiting phase margin minimizes its absolute angle, and the limiting distance is the smallest returned local minimum. Missing crossings or local minima yield an infinite corresponding margin and no associated frequency. Sampled-response analysis covers only the supplied frequency interval.

- Given a square loop model, nonnegative frequencies, and a scalar disk skew, callers obtain disk stability margins at every requested frequency or, by default, their minimum across those frequencies. The skew defaults to zero. Define the skewed sensitivity as the unity-negative-feedback sensitivity plus half of the skew minus one times the identity. For a single channel the disk margin is the reciprocal magnitude of this quantity; for multiple channels it is the reciprocal of the infimum of its largest singular value over positive diagonal similarity scalings. Nonsquare loop models are refused.

- Callers can obtain the system 2-norm and infinity-norm for continuous-time and discrete-time linear models. For stable continuous systems the 2-norm is the square root of the time integral of squared impulse-response magnitudes summed over all channels; for stable discrete systems it is the square root of the sum of squared unit-sample impulse coefficients over all channels and steps. The infinity-norm is the peak singular-value gain over frequency. Unstable systems have infinite 2-norm with a warning, while infinity-norm evaluation can still give a finite peak frequency-response gain for systems without poles on the stability boundary. Continuous-time direct feedthrough gives infinite 2-norm with a warning; boundary poles produce infinite norms with a warning. Stable systems without boundary poles, and without continuous-time direct feedthrough for the 2-norm, give their finite norms without a warning. Other norm orders are refused as unsupported.

- Square linear models can be assessed for passivity in continuous or discrete time. Passivity means that a nonnegative quadratic stored energy exists whose time derivative, or one-step increase in discrete time, never exceeds the supply. With input-feedforward and output-feedback indices both zero by default, the supply is the input-output inner product. With supplied indices, that product is multiplied by one plus the product of the indices, then the input index times squared input magnitude and the output index times squared output magnitude are subtracted. The result distinguishes models that admit this stored-energy bound from those that do not. Callers can obtain the largest input-feedforward index with the output index zero, or the largest output-feedback index with the input index zero, when a finite limiting index exists. Requesting both indices as unknown or using unequal input and output counts is refused.

- A disk of a given margin and skew describes multiplicative gain factors formed by dividing one plus half the margin times one minus the skew times a complex perturbation by one minus half the margin times one plus the skew times that perturbation, for perturbations within the unit circle. For disks with finite positive real-axis intercepts and finite unit-circle intersections, callers obtain the associated gain and phase margins. The gain margin is the smaller symmetric gain increase or decrease reaching the disk boundary on the positive real axis; the phase margin is the symmetric phase rotation reaching the boundary on the unit circle. Callers can request these margins at each frequency or the minimum of each across the supplied frequencies.

- Single-channel rational loop models with isolated real-axis crossings can also report their nonnegative crossing frequencies, together with the signed real response at each crossing. These crossings include positive-real-axis crossings as well as the negative-real-axis crossings used for gain margins. Multiple-channel requests are refused as unsupported.

### FP-10: Pole analysis, root locus, and interactive tuning

- Callers can obtain linear-system poles, single-channel transfer-function zeros, and state-space transmission zeros for square systems. Pole analysis reports natural frequencies as pole magnitudes and damping ratios as the negated real part divided by that magnitude. Discrete models use the principal complex logarithm of each pole divided by the sampling period as the equivalent continuous pole, using one time unit for an unspecified period. Zero equivalent poles have undefined damping. Pole-zero plots place the returned poles and zeros in the complex plane and distinguish them and their originating systems.

- For a single-channel loop model and supplied scalar gains, root locus analysis returns the closed-loop poles for unity negative feedback at each gain. It can also choose a gain range automatically and plot the resulting loci together with open-loop poles and zeros. Multiple-channel root locus requests are refused as unsupported.

- Interactive root locus selection exposes the selected gain and associated closed-loop pole locations. The combined single-channel design view updates loop magnitude and phase responses and closed-loop step response when the selected gain changes. For a two-input, two-output model, the design view supports a caller-specified initial gain: negative feedback connects the first output to the first input, and the displayed step response uses the second input and output. Gain selection by clicking is supported for the single-channel view. Other multiple-channel dimensions are refused by this design view.

- For a plant with one output and one or two inputs, proportional-integral design with zero derivative gain lets callers vary either proportional or integral gain while holding the other fixed. Proportional action multiplies reference-minus-output error; continuous integral action integrates that error, and discrete integral action accumulates the average of consecutive errors times the sampling period. Callers can examine reference tracking or disturbance response and add a feedforward controller acting on the reference. Disturbance is added at the plant input for a one-input plant and drives the second input for a two-input plant. The returned closed-loop model uses the supplied nominal gains; displayed responses use the selected gain perturbation. Nominal gains and feedforward default to zero; the selected term defaults to proportional and its positive perturbation defaults to 0.001. The default step display is reference tracking. The design view is displayed by default and can be suppressed.

### FP-11: Nonlinear operating points and phase-plane analysis

- Given a nonlinear model, an initial state and input guess, and fixed input or desired output values, callers can search for an equilibrium. Continuous-time equilibrium means zero state derivative; discrete-time equilibrium means an unchanged state after one update. For affine models whose fixed constraints give a square nonsingular system of equilibrium equations, the search returns the unique equilibrium. A successful result includes the state, input, and output satisfying the requested conditions. Callers can instead fix selected states, inputs, outputs, or state-update values to find a specified non-equilibrium operating point.

- Operating-point results distinguish successful convergence from unsuccessful search. An unsuccessful search does not expose state and input values as a valid operating point by default; callers explicitly requesting the search result can inspect the unsuccessful candidate and its failure status.

- A nonlinear model can be linearized at supplied state, input, time, and parameter values, whether or not the point is an equilibrium. The resulting linear state-space model represents first-order changes of the supplied dynamics and outputs with respect to state and input and preserves the continuous or discrete timebase.

- For a continuous-time system with two states, callers can plot a phase-plane vector field sampled on a rectangular grid, and trajectories from supplied initial points or points on a rectangular grid, rectangular boundary, or circles. A supplied pair of coordinate sequences defines every Cartesian-product grid point; a boundary grid keeps only points on the rectangle edges. A region and per-axis counts define evenly spaced coordinates including both endpoints. Circular seeds have the requested radius and count around each supplied center. Vector directions reflect the state derivative, and forward, backward, or two-direction trajectories reflect evolution from the chosen points. Models with other state dimensions are refused as nonplanar.

- Phase-plane analysis can locate equilibria from a rectangular search grid and display their locations. For hyperbolic saddle equilibria it can display stable and unstable separatrices along their corresponding directions. Callers can independently enable vector fields, trajectories, equilibria, and separatrices and choose the plotted state-space region.

### FP-12: State-feedback gain design

- For controllable real linear systems and a feasible desired set of closed-loop poles, callers can obtain real state-feedback gains that produce those poles, including conjugate complex poles. The general placement entry refuses a pole multiplicity greater than the rank of the input matrix. Single-input placement also supports repeated poles, and selective placement can retain poles already inside a caller-specified stability region while placing the others. That region consists of poles whose real parts are below the supplied boundary in continuous time, or whose magnitudes are below it in discrete time. An uncontrollable single-input system is refused for full pole assignment.

- For a stabilizable linear model with a well-posed infinite-horizon quadratic regulation objective, callers obtain an objective-minimizing gain applied with a minus sign to state feedback, the cost-to-go matrix, and closed-loop eigenvalues. The instantaneous cost is the state quadratic form plus the input quadratic form plus twice the state-input cross-weighted product. The objective is its integral in continuous time or sum in discrete time. Omitted cross weight is zero, and a supplied model's timebase selects the corresponding problem.

- Quadratic regulation can include integral action on caller-selected linear combinations of states, integrated in continuous time or accumulated once per step in discrete time. The returned gain includes the additional integral-state feedback, and the state-cost dimensions must include those extra states. Incompatible weight or integral-action dimensions and violations of required weight symmetry are refused; a discrete-only design request on a continuous model is refused as incompatible.

### FP-13: Stochastic signals and Kalman estimation

- Given at least two increasing equally spaced sample times and a positive-definite intensity matrix, callers can generate zero-mean multivariate Gaussian white noise. Discrete-time samples have the supplied covariance and are independent across times. For sampled continuous-time noise, covariance multiplied by the sample interval equals the supplied intensity. Nonsquare intensity matrices and nonuniform time grids are refused.

- Callers can obtain auto-correlation or cross-correlation for real sampled signals on a common grid of at least two increasing equally spaced times. A positive lag pairs the first signal at the later time with the second signal at the earlier time. Results distinguish channel pairs and include all integer sample lags with any overlap. Each value is the sum of lagged products times the sample interval divided by the record's elapsed duration; values outside the record contribute zero. Omitting the second signal requests auto-correlation. Signal lengths inconsistent with the time grid and nonuniform sample spacing are refused.

- For a linear model with independent Gaussian process and measurement noise and a well-posed stationary estimation problem, callers obtain the continuous-time or discrete-time Kalman estimator gain, steady-state error covariance, and estimator poles minimizing mean squared state-estimation error. Incompatible covariance dimensions and required symmetry violations are refused. Explicit process/measurement cross-covariance input is refused as unsupported, including an explicitly supplied zero cross-covariance. For the default symmetry validation, the stationary-estimation guarantee is limited to covariance data whose state-space process covariance and measurement covariance remain exactly symmetric in floating-point arithmetic; symmetry lost to rounding may be refused as invalid covariance data.

- A linear model without direct feedthrough can produce a dynamic Kalman estimator that accepts known actuation inputs and measured outputs and returns state estimates while propagating error covariance. Callers supply an initial estimated state and covariance for simulation. Callers can select disturbance channels, or supply their state-influence matrix; measurements use the model output, with caller-selected measured state combinations available when the model exposes its full state. The estimator supports continuous and discrete time. Measurement correction is enabled by default; prediction-only operation propagates the state estimate and covariance without conditioning on measurements. Nonzero direct feedthrough and incompatible measurement dimensions are refused.

- Continuous estimation corrects the predicted state derivative using the measured-minus-predicted output and the covariance-weighted optimal gain; the covariance follows the continuous Riccati differential equation for the supplied process and measurement noise. Discrete estimation returns a one-step prediction: the measurement at the current step corrects the next state prediction, and the covariance is the corresponding next-step prediction-error covariance.

### FP-14: Feedback controller assembly and gain scheduling

- Given a plant and supplied feedback gain, callers can obtain both a controller model and the connected closed-loop model. The default is trajectory tracking: actuation is the desired input minus feedback on actual-minus-desired state. In reference-gain form, actuation combines the caller-supplied reference feedforward contribution with negative state feedback. Caller-selected plant inputs receive the actuation, leaving other inputs available as external disturbances.

- In trajectory-tracking form, integral action accumulates caller-selected linear combinations of actual-minus-desired states. In reference-gain form, it accumulates the selected state combinations minus the corresponding reference components. The supplied integral gain times accumulated error is subtracted from the commanded input. Accumulation uses integration in continuous time and one error contribution per step in discrete time. A supplied estimator replaces actual state feedback with its estimated state and becomes part of the closed loop. Without an estimator, the selected plant outputs must provide the full state used by the controller. Incompatible gain, state-selection, estimator-output, or integral-action dimensions are refused. Discrete integral action is supported for fixed numeric gains.

- For continuous-time plants, gain scheduling accepts gains at caller-supplied operating points and uses selected controller input signals as scheduling coordinates. Nearest-point and linear interpolation support one or multiple scheduling coordinates; cubic interpolation supports one or two coordinates. Linear interpolation is the default. At supplied operating points the corresponding gain is reproduced. Nearest-point interpolation uses the gain at the closest operating point in Euclidean distance. One-coordinate linear interpolation joins consecutive gains by straight segments. One-coordinate cubic interpolation is piecewise cubic with continuous first and second derivatives. Within the covered region, linear and cubic interpolation reproduce gain fields that are affine in the scheduling coordinates. Inconsistent gain shapes or scheduling-coordinate dimensions are refused.

- With at least two scheduling coordinates, a request outside the region covered by gain interpolation uses the gain at the nearest supplied operating point.

- Instead of a numeric gain, the caller can supply a compatible input/output controller that receives actual-minus-desired state error and supplies a signed actuation correction. The assembled controller adds that correction to the desired input and includes the supplied controller's dynamic states in the closed loop.

### FP-15: Robust output-feedback synthesis

- For a continuous-time generalized state-space plant, callers can synthesize a dynamic output-feedback controller minimizing the H2 disturbance-to-performance objective. The caller designates measurement outputs and actuation inputs; the remaining channels are performance outputs and disturbance inputs. The controller maps the measurements directly to the actuation, including the feedback sign in its own dynamics. Supported synthesis problems have stabilizable actuation dynamics, detectable measurement dynamics, full-column-rank direct actuation-to-performance influence, full-row-rank direct disturbance-to-measurement influence, and no imaginary-axis invariant zeros in those two channels. H2 synthesis requires zero direct disturbance-to-performance and actuation-to-measurement feedthrough. The returned controller stabilizes the interconnection and minimizes the summed squared impulse-response energy from disturbances to performance outputs.

- Under the same stabilizability, detectability, rank, and invariant-zero conditions, continuous-time H-infinity synthesis minimizes the achievable worst-case gain and produces a stabilizing dynamic output-feedback controller, the resulting disturbance-to-performance closed-loop model, and its achieved worst-case gain. Direct feedthrough is permitted when the resulting feedback equations are well posed and a finite achievable gain exists. The reported gain is the peak singular-value gain of the returned closed-loop model.

- Mixed-sensitivity design accepts a plant and any nonempty selection of weights on tracking error, actuation effort, and plant output. Its generalized plant takes reference and actuation inputs, returns the weighted reference-minus-plant-output error, weighted actuation, and weighted plant output for the supplied weights, and exposes the tracking error as the controller measurement. Synthesis returns a controller and closed-loop model minimizing the combined weighted H-infinity objective in unity negative feedback. Supported weights are square multichannel weights and single-channel weights applied independently and equally to corresponding channels. The plant itself may have unequal input and output counts. Incompatible weight dimensions and a request with no weights are refused.

### FP-16: Model simplification and identification

- Minimal realization removes uncontrollable or unobservable state dynamics, or cancels matching transfer-function poles and zeros, preserving the remaining input/output behavior. Callers can set a positive cancellation tolerance; pole-zero pairs separated by less than that tolerance cancel, and pairs separated by more than that tolerance remain distinct. The result exposes the reduced state or polynomial order.

- A caller can keep or eliminate selected states, inputs, and outputs of a state-space model. State truncation removes the specified state contributions; continuous-time equilibrium matching returns the retained-state dynamics and outputs with eliminated states constrained to their unique instantaneous equilibrium for the current retained state and input. Equilibrium matching is the default. Conflicting keep and eliminate specifications, a singular eliminated subsystem for equilibrium matching, and discrete-time equilibrium matching are refused.

- Stable continuous-time models expose Hankel singular values in descending order: the square roots of the eigenvalues of the product of controllability and observability Gramians. Continuous-time balanced reduction produces models of requested lower orders, by truncation by default or by preserving zero-frequency gain. Truncation keeps the direct feedthrough unchanged. For stable systems, truncation bounds the infinity-norm approximation error by twice the sum of discarded Hankel singular values. For systems with unstable modes, the supported requested orders are at least the number of unstable modes, and reduction retains those modes.

- Discrete-time impulse-response observations can produce a state-space realization of a caller-selected order. With the default observation window, at least four times the requested order plus one samples per input/output channel are required, and fewer are refused. For exact data from a minimal finite-order linear model, with requested order equal to that model order and enough observations to identify all modes, the realization reproduces its impulse response. At a sampling period other than one, this reconstruction promise applies to strictly proper models and unit-area impulse data; unit-period data also supports direct feedthrough. The returned sampling period is the supplied period, or unspecified discrete time when omitted. Returned singular values describe the finite impulse-data Hankel matrix with twice the requested order block rows and columns: each block is the impulse sample indexed by the sum of its zero-based block row and column plus one.

- Caller-supplied input/output records can produce a requested number of discrete impulse-response coefficients. The count defaults to the record length. The coefficients minimize squared output prediction error under causal convolution with the recorded inputs and zero input before the record, or with the initial coefficient-count samples omitted when requested. Returned coefficients use unit-area impulse normalization: convolution predictions multiply them by the sampling period. The period defaults to the response record's sample spacing, or one for bare input/output records. Input and output record lengths must agree. Fewer usable observations than the coefficient count times the number of inputs produces an underdetermined-estimate warning; with enough usable observations the estimate is returned without a warning.

### FP-17: Describing functions and nonlinear oscillation analysis

- For positive excitation amplitudes, callers can obtain the complex fundamental-harmonic gain of a scalar nonlinearity under sinusoidal excitation, with magnitude equal to output fundamental amplitude divided by input amplitude and phase equal to the fundamental phase shift. Caller-supplied nonlinearities, symmetric saturation, relay with hysteresis, and friction backlash are supported. Negative amplitudes are refused. For a relay with positive hysteresis threshold, excitation smaller than that threshold yields an undefined describing gain. For backlash, excitation no larger than half the play width yields zero describing gain. For nonzero finite gains, numerical error in complex magnitude is at most 0.002 plus 0.002 times the magnitude of the mathematical gain.

- Saturation clips to supplied lower and upper limits. With only one bound, the bounds are symmetric with that absolute magnitude; the default magnitude is one. A hysteretic relay with positive output magnitude and positive symmetric threshold magnitude starts at its negative output level, switches positive strictly above the positive threshold and negative strictly below the negative threshold, and retains its previous output between and at the thresholds. Friction backlash with positive play width starts with zero output. The output remains unchanged while the input is within half the play width of the previous output; outside that interval it follows the input with an offset of half the width toward the previous output.

- Given a linear model, a nonlinearity, and amplitude and frequency ranges, describing-function analysis identifies candidate oscillation amplitudes and frequencies at intersections between the linear Nyquist response and the negative reciprocal of the nonlinearity's describing function. Results distinguish detected intersections from no intersections and can plot both curves with their intersection locations. For isolated intersections within the supplied amplitude and frequency ranges, errors in amplitude, frequency, and complex-plane position are each at most 0.002 plus 0.002 times the magnitude of the corresponding mathematical value. The same accuracy applies to the finite negative-reciprocal describing-gain curve.

### FP-18: Differentially flat trajectory planning

- Callers can describe a differentially flat system using forward and inverse mappings between state/input values and flat outputs with their derivatives. Evaluation applies the supplied mappings and parameter values. A controllable continuous-time single-input, single-output linear state-space model can also be converted to a flat representation; multiple-channel and noncontrollable linear models are refused for that conversion.

- Given a flat model, initial and final states and inputs, a start and end time, and a trajectory basis capable of satisfying those independent endpoint conditions, point-to-point planning returns an evaluable state and input trajectory meeting the endpoints and the flat-system relations. The total number of trajectory coefficients must be at least twice the sum of the state and input counts; a smaller basis is refused. A basis with separate variables must have one variable per flat output. For either planning form, when no basis is supplied, each flat output uses polynomial powers with twice the sum of the state and input counts as its number of basis elements.

- With remaining trajectory freedom, point-to-point planning accepts a running cost on a uniformly spaced time grid with at least one interior sample and with zero running cost at the prescribed initial state and input. Bounded linear or nonlinear state/input path constraints apply at the interior samples; endpoint states and inputs are specified separately. A successful optimized trajectory satisfies the endpoint conditions and sampled constraints at a local minimum of the supplied objective within the selected trajectory family. For a feasible convex quadratic objective with linear constraints on the trajectory coefficients, it returns a minimizing trajectory. When the total coefficient count equals twice the sum of the state and input counts, the product warns and returns the endpoint trajectory without applying the requested cost or path constraints. Running cost is the time integral of the sampled costs held constant from each time point to the next.

- Flat-system optimal planning can instead fix only the initial state and input and optimize running and terminal costs, with sampled state/input constraints. This requires more trajectory coefficients than the sum of the state and input counts and at least one running or terminal cost; requests not meeting these conditions are refused. Running costs are integrated as samples held constant to the next supplied time, and terminal cost uses the final state and input. Optimized point-to-point and initial-only trajectories expose success and achieved cost; unsuccessful optimization produces a warning and unsuccessful status with an evaluable finite candidate retaining the prescribed initial state and input, while a successful optimization completes without a warning. Evaluation can return inputs, states, and model outputs as a time response when the model provides an output law.

### FP-19: Finite-horizon optimal controller design

- Given a continuous-time or discrete-time input/output model, an initial state, a finite time horizon, a running cost, and optional terminal cost, callers can compute an optimized input and state trajectory and its achieved cost. The continuous-time objective is the integral of the piecewise-linear sampled running cost plus the terminal cost. The discrete-time objective sums running costs at the steps preceding the terminal time and adds the terminal cost. The result distinguishes successful optimization from failure to converge; nonlinear problems can yield a local solution, and no global optimum or universal convergence is promised.

- Callers can constrain states and inputs with bounded linear expressions or bounded nonlinear expressions at the supplied time points, and apply separate terminal constraints. Cost and constraint conveniences support quadratic state/input tracking penalties, state ranges and polytopes, input ranges and polytopes, and output ranges and polytopes. Tracking cost is the sum of the state-error and input-error quadratic forms with the supplied weights and reference values, which default to zero. A scalar weight applies equally to each corresponding signal; an omitted weight removes that term. Range constraints include both bounds, and polytope constraints bound each supplied linear combination from above. A successful result obeys the sampled constraints. Weights, bounds, and constraint dimensions inconsistent with the modeled signals are refused.

- The input trajectory may use a caller-selected trajectory basis and coefficients, or independently specified values at the supplied time points. The selected trajectory family restricts the admissible inputs, and the returned input values represent that family. Callers can supply an initial trajectory guess; incompatible initial-guess dimensions are refused.

- For feasible linear discrete-time models with positive-definite quadratic input penalties, convex quadratic state penalties, and linear constraints, the product returns a solution attaining the finite-horizon quadratic actuation optimum. For nonlinear models, the returned state trajectory is consistent with the supplied dynamics, initial condition, and optimized input at the requested times. Optimization failure produces a warning and an unsuccessful result with its reason and a finite candidate trajectory retaining the prescribed initial state and discrete dynamics for discrete-time models; a successful optimization completes without a warning.

- A discrete-time finite-horizon actuation problem can produce a model predictive controller that accepts the current state and returns the first action of the optimized horizon. Subsequent state measurements lead to actions for the updated initial state, and the controller can participate in feedback simulation. Creating this controller for a continuous-time model is refused as unsupported.

### FP-20: Finite-horizon and moving-horizon estimation

- Given a continuous-time or discrete-time model, a finite history of measured outputs and known controls, and an estimation cost, callers can estimate state, process-disturbance, and measurement-noise histories consistent with the model and observations. Caller-selected input channels distinguish known actuation from unknown disturbance. The result includes the achieved cost and distinguishes successful estimation from unsuccessful optimization. Running estimation cost is the integral of the piecewise-linear sampled costs in continuous time, and the sum of costs over all supplied sample times in discrete time.

- Estimation accepts a prior initial-state estimate and an initial-state penalty on disagreement with that prior, trajectory constraints, and bounds on disturbances. Gaussian likelihood cost is the sum of the disturbance quadratic form weighted by inverse disturbance covariance and the measurement-noise quadratic form weighted by inverse measurement covariance. Omitting one covariance omits its cost term; at least one is supplied. For a linear discrete-time model with a strictly convex quadratic estimation objective and feasible linear constraints, the product returns an estimate minimizing that objective; nonlinear estimation may return a local solution.

- Measurement and actuation histories must match their selected signal counts and the supplied time points; initial trajectory guesses must match the estimated state and disturbance histories. Incompatible shapes are refused. Unsuccessful optimization produces a warning and an unsuccessful result with its reason, rather than presenting the candidate as a converged estimate; a successful estimation completes without a warning.

- A discrete-time estimation problem whose known actuation channels precede a disjoint set of disturbance channels can produce a moving-horizon estimator accepting measurements and known inputs. Each new sample replaces the oldest sample in the configured window. When no initial estimator state is supplied, the initial output is zero, and measurement, known-input, and estimated-state histories preceding the first supplied sample are zero. The previous estimated state at the new window start supplies the prior for that window. The output is the one-step state prediction from the most recent fitted state and input. A continuous-time model is refused for moving-horizon estimator creation.
